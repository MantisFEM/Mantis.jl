Why Mantis? ​

Mantis aims to provide a research toolbox for structure-preserving discretisations with a focus on differential forms.

Differential forms (see here) provide a generalised formalism for working with operators like the gradient, curl, and divergence in arbitrary dimensions and on general manifolds. From a finite element (see here) perspective, this allows one to derive structure-preserving discretisation, study their properties, and do all of this in a general setting.

Unfortunately, while differential forms provide a powerful theoretical framework, their formalisms are often absent in FEM codes. This is precisely the gap that Mantis aims to fill.

Working with differential forms and structure-preserving finite elements is currently mostly done by researchers (and their students). As a result, this is also the target audience for Mantis.

Room for experimentation ​

In our opinion, a code for researchers should be easy to adapt and extend. After all, researchers often want to experiment with things that are not (yet) standard. Using existing codes, especially closed-source or multi-language ones, often create a barier in this regard. That is why Mantis is open-source and written in Julia (more on why we choose Julia here). This allows you to extend Mantis-functionality whenever you need. And, if you think that such an extensions may be useful to other, feel free to contribute.

Since Mantis is aimed mostly at researchers and students, we try to avoid forcing certain choices upon the user. We do try to provide helper functions for making standard choices, though there is no requirement to use them.

'Blackboxability' & Abstraction Layers ​

Mantis is designed with research in mind. Research often focusses on one part of a problem at a time. For example, when researching new finite element spaces, one focusses on precisely this aspect. How do you construct this space, and what are its properties? Nevertheless, once you have some prototype for the space, you will likely want to test it in a finite element simulation. This requires all other aspects of a finite element code, but is something you probably prefer not to have to reimplement. Mantis allows you to write your new space as a FunctionSpaces.AbstractFESpace, allowing you to focus on implementing the new space. Every other module and function will understand how to deal with this new space out-of-the-box. This 'blackboxability' applies to each module of Mantis and is there for precisely this usecase.

Additionally, abstract types and functions defined for them, allow us to create multiple abstraction layers. This allows us to create different entrypoints into Mantis. Whether you want to quickly setup some well-known spaces and solve a simple problem, or you want to have fine-grained control over one or more steps, the abstraction layers can help do (some) of the heavy lifting. Working with functions on abstract types and relying on Julia's multiple dispatch to select the most appropriate algorithm is also ideal for differential forms. With forms, we often work with general concepts that have specific incarnations in specific scenarios. Such abstraction layers allow us, and you, to turn these concepts into code.
